Savings accounts, provident funds, government securities, and corporate bonds are not the same type of instrument. Some are bank deposit contracts; others are government-backed retirement or small-savings schemes; while others are debt instruments through which governments, banks, or corporations borrow capital from investors and pay periodic interest. Their investment tenures, liquidity conditions, tax treatments, and underlying risk profiles vary substantially.

## 1. Deposits

### Fixed Deposit (FD)

A Fixed Deposit involves parking a lump sum with a bank or financial institution for a specified tenure. The interest rate and payout frequency (e.g., monthly, quarterly, or cumulative at maturity) are locked in when the account is opened. Premature withdrawals typically incur an interest rate penalty or administrative fees. Always review auto-renewal settings and maturity instructions.

Eligible deposits in insured banks are protected by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC). Under current DICGC guidelines, bank deposits (including principal and interest across all branches of the same bank held in the same right and capacity) are insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor. Always confirm whether the issuing bank and deposit type are covered.

### Recurring Deposit (RD)

A Recurring Deposit allows individuals to accumulate savings over a set period by depositing a fixed amount each month. The monthly due date, late payment penalties, premature closure terms, and maturity interest are governed by bank or Post Office rules. An RD is particularly convenient for salaried individuals building a disciplined monthly savings habit.

## 2. Provident Funds and Small-Savings Schemes

The schemes in this section do not share a single statutory structure. While EPF and PPF are structured provident funds, Sukanya Samriddhi and Ponmagan are specialized small-savings accounts. Eligibility, annual contribution limits, interest rates, and withdrawal rules vary by scheme; always consult current official guidelines.

### Employees' Provident Fund (EPF)

EPF is a mandatory retirement savings scheme for salaried employees working in eligible establishments. Both the employee and employer contribute designated percentages of basic wages under the supervision of the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO), with statutory interest credited annually. Specific rules govern transfer between employers via the Universal Account Number (UAN), partial non-refundable advances (e.g., for housing or medical emergencies), and final superannuation settlements. Contribution thresholds and tax rules depend on current statutory provisions.

Estimate your accumulated corpus at retirement based on salary growth, contributions, and interest using our [EPF Calculator](../../calculators/epf_calculator.html).

### Public Provident Fund (PPF)

The Public Provident Fund is a sovereign-backed long-term savings scheme open to all resident Indian individuals. Annual contribution caps (minimum ₹500, maximum ₹1.5 lakh per financial year), a 15-year statutory tenure, tax-exempt interest (EEE status), partial withdrawal windows, and loan facilities are defined under Government of India savings rules. Because PPF carries a long lock-in period, plan your liquidity needs accordingly.

### Sukanya Samriddhi Account (SSA)

A government-backed small-savings scheme initiated by a parent or legal guardian for an eligible girl child before she attains 10 years of age. Unlike standard provident funds, it carries specific rules regarding entry age, contribution limits, partial withdrawals for higher education, and maturity upon marriage or completion of 21 years from account opening. The interest rate is reviewed and notified quarterly by the Ministry of Finance.

### Ponmagan Podhuvaippu Nidhi

"Ponmagan" is not an alias or male counterpart to Sukanya Samriddhi Account. It refers to a specialized savings initiative offered within the Tamil Nadu Postal Circle; do not assume it is a uniform nationwide Central Government provident fund scheme. Verify current availability, eligibility criteria, interest rates, and withdrawal norms directly with your local Head Post Office or postal division.

## 3. National Savings Certificates and Kisan Vikas Patra (NSC / KVP)

National Savings Certificate (NSC) and Kisan Vikas Patra (KVP) are sovereign-backed small-savings certificates distributed primarily through India Post branches and authorized banks. They are distinct from market-traded corporate securities. The contracted interest rate at the time of purchase remains locked for the entire tenure. Rules governing tenure, premature encashment, transferability, and tax deductions (e.g., Section 80C eligibility for NSC) are defined by applicable government notifications. The doubling period and interest rate for KVP are periodically revised by the Ministry of Finance; always refer to the latest official India Post notifications rather than outdated marketing materials.

## 4. Government Securities and PSU Bonds

### Treasury Bills and Government Securities (G-Secs)

Treasury Bills (T-Bills) are short-term zero-coupon debt instruments issued by the Central Government with tenures of 91, 182, or 364 days; they are issued at a discount and redeemed at face value. Central Government dated securities and State Development Loans (SDLs) are long-term bonds paying semi-annual coupons. While sovereign instruments carry zero default risk, selling them in the secondary market prior to maturity exposes investors to interest rate risk and price fluctuations.

RBI Floating Rate Savings Bonds (FRSB) are retail savings bonds issued by the Government of India and administered through the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Their interest rate is semi-floating (pegged 0.35% above the NSC rate) and paid half-yearly. Retail individual investors can purchase and trade eligible government securities directly without intermediaries via the **RBI Retail Direct** portal.

### Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) Bonds

PSU bonds are debt securities issued by state-owned enterprises or public sector undertakings to raise capital. Do not assume that a bond carries a sovereign government guarantee simply because the issuer is a PSU. Verify whether the specific tranche carries an explicit government guarantee, review the credit rating (e.g., AAA/AA+), and check the coupon rate, tenure, and liquidity in secondary markets.

## 5. Corporate Debt Instruments (Bonds, Debentures, and NCDs)

Corporate debt securities are debt instruments issued by private and public companies to borrow funds from investors. Their terminology—such as debentures, bonds, and Non-Convertible Debentures (NCDs)—reflects differences in security status (secured vs. unsecured), coupon payment structure, tenure, and secondary market listing. If the issuing company's financial health deteriorates, investors face credit risk, including the risk of delayed interest or permanent loss of principal. Credit ratings provide useful assessments but are not sovereign guarantees. Liquidating before maturity in secondary markets may also involve price discounts or liquidity shortages.

A **Certificate of Deposit (CD)** is a short-term, negotiable money-market instrument issued strictly by eligible commercial banks and select financial institutions. It should not be confused with corporate bonds or company fixed deposits. Always review the offering memorandum to determine the issuer, eligible investor categories, tradability, and settlement terms.

## Key Factors to Consider When Comparing Instruments

- **Tenure & Liquidity:** How long can you afford to lock in your funds? What penalties, fees, or capital losses apply if you need to withdraw early?
- **Interest Rate Structure:** Is the interest rate fixed for life, revised periodically by government notification, or determined by prevailing secondary market yields?
- **Maturity & Taxation:** What is the net post-tax return? Are returns taxed at income tax slab rates, or do they qualify for special exemptions (such as Section 80C or EEE status)?
- **Credit & Default Risk:** Bank deposits, sovereign government schemes, and corporate bonds carry fundamentally different risk profiles. DICGC deposit insurance covers eligible bank deposits up to ₹5 lakh per depositor—it does not cover corporate bonds, debentures, or mutual funds.
- **Sovereign Guarantee Verification:** If a product is marketed as "government-backed," confirm whether an explicit sovereign guarantee exists in the official prospectus or statutory rules.

Interest rates, contribution ceilings, tax treatment, and eligibility criteria are subject to periodic regulatory updates. Before committing funds, review the latest official program guidelines to ensure they align with your investment horizon and financial goals.
